# Preface {#preface .unnumbered}

## To the Student {#to-student}

This book is an introduction to the scientific study of the mind. It is written for a reader who has never taken a
course in the subject and who wants to understand, carefully and from the evidence, what happens to a piece of
information between the moment it reaches a person and the moment it changes what that person does next.

The book follows the building it was written for. Callow School teaches the mind on seven floors, one subject to a
floor, and each floor's teacher writes the lesson on a board in five short parts. Those thirty-five board entries are
deliberately brief: a board is something to read from the back of a room. This textbook keeps every one of them,
word for word, and then does what a board cannot. It explains each idea, shows where it came from, reports the
research that established it, and marks the places where the evidence is more complicated than a sentence in chalk
can be.

The title is a small act of homage. William James called his great survey of 1890 *The Principles of Psychology*, and
it opens its chapter on attention with the remark that "everyone knows what attention is." This book shares his
conviction that the things everyone already knows about their own minds are exactly the things most worth
examining.

## What This Book Teaches {#what-it-teaches}

The seven chapters follow the order of the floors, and the order is not arbitrary. Read upward, the building traces
what happens to a piece of information: it is **named**, **noticed**, **kept**, **sorted**, **changed**, **bent** and
**corrected**.

**Chapter 1, Language,** comes first because everything else is taught in words. It examines how a word points at a
thing, how words are built from smaller meaningful pieces, how order carries meaning, how context settles which of
several meanings a word carries, and why English spelling is so irregular.

**Chapter 2, Attention,** concerns what gets processed at all. Attention is limited, it can be directed in advance,
it finds some things effortlessly and others only by search, it switches at a cost, and it can miss what is in plain
view.

**Chapter 3, Memory,** follows what is kept. It treats memory as reconstruction rather than recording, and covers
forgetting, interference, the reshaping of a memory each time it is recalled, and the robust advantage of spacing
study over time.

**Chapter 4, Categories,** concerns how the mind sorts. Real categories have central members and blurred edges, are
organised at several levels, depend on which features are attended to, and always leave a remainder.

**Chapter 5, Learning,** describes how experience changes the brain itself: cells joined at synapses whose strength
changes with use, thresholds that decide whether a cell fires, the indispensable role of feedback, and the pruning of
connections during development.

**Chapter 6, Bias,** catalogues the systematic ways judgment departs from what the evidence warrants: framing,
anchoring, confirmation, hindsight, and the informal fallacies of argument.

**Chapter 7, Error,** closes the sequence with the idea that learning is driven by the gap between what was expected
and what occurred. It treats surprise, correction, the size of each step, the value of noticed mistakes, and the
cost of certainty.

A Conclusion draws the chapters together and turns their findings into advice for studying, including for the
examination the school sets. A Glossary and a list of References close the book.

## How to Use This Book {#how-to-use}

Each chapter has the same parts, so that a reader always knows where to look.

- **The placard.** Every chapter opens with the placard that stands by its floor's stairs, stating in two sentences
  what the floor is for.
- **Learning objectives.** A short list of what you should be able to do when you have finished the chapter. Return
  to it afterwards and test yourself against it.
- **On the board.** Each section begins with one of the five board entries, quoted exactly and set on slate. *This is
  what the school's examination covers.* The text that follows the board explains it; the board itself is what to
  study.
- **Key terms** are printed in **bold** where they are defined, and collected in the Glossary with the section in
  which each appears.
- **Research Spotlight** boxes describe a single landmark study in detail: who did it, what they did, what they
  found, and why it mattered.
- **Try It** boxes give a short exercise you can do with a pencil or a friend, so that you experience a finding
  rather than only read about it.
- **A Closer Look** boxes mark places where the evidence is contested, or where a popular version of a finding goes
  further than the research does.
- **Chapter review.** Each chapter ends with a summary, its key terms, review questions and suggestions for further
  reading.

# Language {#language}

{{placard 1F}}

::: objectives
[Learning Objectives]{.bh}

1. Distinguish a word from the thing it refers to, and explain why the link between them is arbitrary.
2. Identify the morphemes in a complex word and use them to interpret words you have never seen.
3. Explain how word order carries meaning in English, and how other languages carry the same information.
4. Describe how listeners resolve lexical and structural ambiguity, and how quickly they do so.
5. Explain why English spelling is irregular, and what that irregularity costs a learner.
:::

Language is the first subject in the building for a practical reason, which the placard states plainly: everything
else is taught in words. Before a student can be told about attention or memory, the student needs words for them,
and needs to understand what words are and how they work.

The scientific study of language and the mind, **psycholinguistics**, treats a language as a layered system. Sounds
combine into meaningful pieces; pieces combine into words; words combine, according to rules, into sentences; and
sentences mean things in context. The linguist Charles Hockett (1960) listed the "design features" that set human
language apart from other systems of communication. Three of them organise this chapter: **arbitrariness** (the form
of a word does not resemble its meaning), **duality of patterning** (meaningless units such as sounds and letters
combine into meaningful ones), and **productivity** (a finite set of pieces and rules yields an unlimited number of new
expressions).

## 1.1 Names {#names}

{{board 1F NAMES}}

A word is a **sign**, and the Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure, in lectures published after his death as the
*Course in General Linguistics* (1916), divided the sign into two parts: the **signifier**, the sound or written form,
and the **signified**, the concept it calls up. His central claim was that the bond between the two is
**arbitrary**. Nothing about the sounds of *tree* resembles a tree, which is why the same concept is *arbre* in French
and *Baum* in German. The few exceptions, such as **onomatopoeia** (*buzz*, *hiss*), are fewer and less consistent
across languages than one might expect.

Because the bond is arbitrary, a name works exactly as the board says: as a handle. It allows us to refer to a thing
in its absence, to talk about it, plan around it and pass it to someone else, without the thing being present. And
because the bond is conventional, the handle can be changed without changing the thing. Shakespeare's Juliet makes the
point in a single line: "a rose by any other name would smell as sweet."

Philosophers sharpened the idea. Gottlob Frege (1892) distinguished a name's **reference**, the object it picks out,
from its **sense**, the way it presents that object. The "morning star" and the "evening star" have different senses
but the same reference: both are the planet Venus. Discovering that they named one object was a discovery about the
sky, not about the words. Renaming changes the sense, the route by which we reach a thing; it does not change the
thing reached.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · A Name Changes the Memory, Not the Thing]{.bh}
Carmichael, Hogan and Walter (1932) showed participants simple, ambiguous line drawings, each introduced with one of
two names. The same drawing was called "eyeglasses" for some participants and "dumbbells" for others. Asked later to
reproduce the drawings from memory, participants tended to draw them to resemble the name they had been given. The
drawings themselves had not changed at all; what changed was what people kept of them. The finding refines the
board: renaming a thing leaves it exactly what it was, but a name can still shape what a mind stores about it, a
theme Chapter 3 takes up in detail.
:::

## 1.2 Pieces {#pieces}

{{board 1F PIECES}}

The smallest unit of language that carries meaning is the **morpheme**. *Unreadable* contains three: the prefix *un-*
("not"), the root *read*, and the suffix *-able* ("can be done"). Morphemes that can stand alone as words, like
*read*, are **free morphemes**; those that cannot, like *un-* and *-able*, are **bound morphemes**.

Linguists distinguish two kinds of bound morpheme. **Inflectional morphemes** mark grammatical information without
changing a word's basic category: the *-s* of *daemons*, the *-ed* of *walked*. **Derivational morphemes** build new
words, often of a new category: *-able* turns the verb *read* into the adjective *readable*, and *-ness* turns that
into the noun *readableness*.

The board's promise, that knowing the pieces lets you read words nobody taught you, is the property Hockett called
productivity, and it is one of the best-established facts in the study of children's language.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · The Wug Test]{.bh}
Jean Berko (1958) showed young children a drawing of a small bird-like creature and said, "This is a wug." She then
showed two of them: "Now there are two of them. There are two..." Children readily answered "wugs", pronouncing the
plural ending correctly, although they could never have heard the word before, because Berko had invented it. They
did the same with invented verbs ("he ricks every day; yesterday he...") and invented adjectives. The children were
not repeating forms they had memorised. They had learned the rules for combining pieces, and applied them to a word
that was new.
:::

Morphology matters well beyond the nursery. The written English that schoolchildren meet contains far more distinct
words than could ever be taught one at a time; Nagy and Anderson (1984) estimated the number of distinct word
families in printed school English at roughly 88,500. Most of the vocabulary a reader acquires is therefore learned
indirectly, and much of it by recognising known pieces in unfamiliar combinations. Readers who are aware of
morphological structure tend to learn new words more readily.

::: {.box .tryit}
[Try It]{.bh}
Divide each word into its morphemes and say what each piece contributes: *misremembered*, *rethinkable*,
*unforgettably*, *predictable*. Then invent a word that no dictionary contains, built only from real pieces (for
example *unrecategorisable*), and ask a friend what it means. If they can tell you, you have just observed
productivity at work.
:::

## 1.3 Order {#order}

{{board 1F ORDER}}

**Syntax** is the system of rules by which words combine into phrases and sentences. The board's two sentences
contain exactly the same three words, yet they describe opposite events. What differs is which noun fills which
**thematic role**: the **agent**, who performs the action, and the **patient**, who undergoes it. In English, the
default order of a simple sentence is subject, verb, object, and the position of a noun before or after the verb is
the main signal of its role.

Not every language relies on order to the same degree. Latin marks roles with endings on the nouns themselves, a
system called **case**. *Canis hominem mordet* and *Hominem canis mordet* both mean "the dog bites the man", because
the ending *-em* marks *hominem* as the object wherever it stands. English once had a rich case system too, and as it
lost most of its endings during the medieval period, word order took over their work.

Order and meaning are separable, and Noam Chomsky (1957) made the point with a famous pair. *Colorless green ideas
sleep furiously* is nonsense, yet it is recognisably a grammatical English sentence. *Furiously sleep ideas green
colorless*, the same words reversed, is not. Speakers can judge structure independently of meaning, which suggests
that the rules of order are a distinct part of what they know.

Order also explains a characteristic stumble in reading. In a **garden-path sentence**, the early words invite one
structure and the later words require another. Thomas Bever's (1970) example is *The horse raced past the barn fell.*
Most readers first take *raced* as the main verb, reach *fell*, and have to go back: the sentence means "the horse
that was raced past the barn fell." Nothing was added and nothing was taken away. The reader had simply assigned the
words the wrong structure, and repairing it takes measurable time.

## 1.4 Ambiguity {#ambiguity}

{{board 1F AMBIGUITY}}

**Lexical ambiguity** occurs when a single word form carries more than one meaning. Linguists distinguish
**homonymy**, in which the meanings are unrelated and only share a form (the *bank* of a river and the *bank* that
holds money), from **polysemy**, in which the meanings are related senses of one word (a *paper* as a material and a
*paper* as a newspaper). The board's *box*, a container in one sentence and a square to be ticked in the other, is a
case of polysemy: both senses descend from the idea of an enclosed space.

How does a listener arrive at the intended meaning? A natural guess is that context is consulted first and only the
appropriate meaning retrieved. The evidence suggests something more surprising.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · Both Meanings, Then One]{.bh}
David Swinney (1979) used a technique called **cross-modal priming**. Participants listened to sentences such as one
about a room in which "spiders, roaches and other bugs" were found, a context that clearly favours the insect
meaning of *bugs*. At the very moment *bugs* was heard, a string of letters appeared on a screen, and participants
decided as quickly as possible whether it was a word. They recognised *ant* faster than an unrelated word, as
expected; but they also recognised *spy* faster, although the sentence had nothing to do with spying. When the letters
appeared a few syllables later, only the contextually appropriate meaning was still primed. For a brief moment, both
meanings had been active, and context then selected one.
:::

Swinney's results, and similar findings by others, suggest that word recognition first makes several meanings
available and that context then selects among them, typically within a fraction of a second. Later work showed that
the balance depends on how frequent each meaning is and how strongly the context constrains it. The board's last
line is therefore correct in a precise sense: the words around a word do decide which meaning was intended, but they
do so by selecting among candidates the mind has briefly considered.

Ambiguity is not limited to single words. In **structural ambiguity**, the words are clear but their grouping is not:
*I saw the man with the telescope* can mean that the telescope was used for seeing or that the man was holding it.
Here too, context and plausibility settle the matter, usually so quickly that the listener never notices there was a
choice.

## 1.5 Spelling {#spelling}

{{board 1F SPELLING}}

A writing system in which letters stand for sounds rests on the **alphabetic principle**. The system of spelling
conventions for a particular language is its **orthography**. Orthographies differ in how consistently they map
sounds to letters, a property described by the **orthographic depth** hypothesis (Katz & Frost, 1992). In a
**shallow orthography**, such as Finnish or Italian, a reader who knows the rules can pronounce nearly any written word
correctly. In a **deep orthography**, such as English, the same letters represent different sounds in different words
(*through*, *though*, *tough*, *cough*), and the same sound is spelled in different ways.

The board is right that the rule is old and full of exceptions, and the history explains why. English spelling began
to settle in the late fifteenth century, after William Caxton set up his printing press at Westminster in 1476, and it
was fixed more firmly by the dictionaries of the eighteenth century, most famously Samuel Johnson's of 1755. During
the same centuries, the pronunciation of English vowels was changing dramatically in a process called the **Great
Vowel Shift**, which ran roughly from 1400 to 1700. Spelling was frozen while the sounds kept moving. Many of the
"silent" letters of modern English, such as the *k* of *knight*, were once pronounced.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · What a Deep Orthography Costs]{.bh}
Seymour, Aro and Erskine (2003) compared children learning to read in thirteen European orthographies. Near the end
of their first year of instruction, children in most of the languages could read familiar words and simple invented
words with high accuracy. Children learning English lagged far behind, and the researchers estimated that
establishing basic reading skill in English takes more than twice as long as in the shallowest orthographies. The
difference lies not in the children but in the rule they are asked to learn.
:::

Finally, the note in smaller chalk. The verb *spell*, "to name the letters of a word", reached English from Old French
*espeller*, which came in turn from a Germanic word meaning to tell or relate. The noun *spell*, an incantation,
descends from Old English *spell*, meaning a story or a saying, which survives in *gospel*, "good news". The two words
share their distant Germanic ancestry. The connection between writing and magic runs through another word as well:
*glamour*, which once meant a magical enchantment, is a Scottish alteration of *grammar*, from an old belief that
learning, and the learned, had occult power. Whoever added the chalk note was wrong about the spelling rule, which
casts no spell on anyone. They were not entirely wrong about the word.

# Attention {#attention}

{{placard 2F}}

::: objectives
[Learning Objectives]{.bh}

1. Explain why attention is described as a limited capacity, and describe early theories of selection.
2. Describe the spatial cueing paradigm and the costs and benefits of valid and invalid cues.
3. Distinguish feature search from conjunction search, and explain the pop-out effect.
4. Explain the evidence that apparent multitasking consists of rapid switching between tasks.
5. Describe inattentional blindness and change blindness, and why people underestimate them.
:::

"Everyone knows what attention is," William James wrote in 1890. "It is the taking possession by the mind, in clear
and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought." The placard
gives the reason this chapter precedes the next: you cannot keep what you never noticed. **Attention** is the set of
processes by which some information is selected for further processing at the expense of other information, and the
phrase "at the expense of" is the heart of the subject.

## 2.1 Limits {#limits}

{{board 2F LIMITS}}

Modern research on attention began with the problem of the **cocktail party**: how can a listener follow one
conversation in a crowded room? Colin Cherry (1953) studied it with **dichotic listening**, presenting a different
message to each ear through headphones and asking participants to **shadow** one message, repeating it aloud as they
heard it. They could do so well, but they could report almost nothing of the message in the other ear. They noticed
if the unattended voice changed from male to female, but not if it switched language or was played backwards.

Donald Broadbent (1958) proposed that the mind contains a **filter** that selects one channel early, on the basis of
physical features such as location or pitch, and blocks the rest before their meaning is analysed. The theory soon
met difficulties. Neville Moray (1959) found that about a third of participants noticed their own name in the
unattended ear, which a filter that blocked meaning should not allow. Anne Treisman (1960) proposed instead that the
filter **attenuates** unattended input, turning it down rather than off, so that especially important or expected
words can still break through.

A different tradition described attention not as a gate but as a resource. In *Attention and Effort*, Daniel Kahneman
(1973) proposed a limited pool of **capacity** that can be allocated among tasks, with harder tasks drawing more. This
is the board's account: attention is not added, only moved. Devoting more to one thing leaves less for everything
else, and the demand of a task, not merely the number of tasks, determines what remains.

## 2.2 Cues {#cues}

{{board 2F CUES}}

Attention can be directed before anything happens. Michael Posner (1980) developed the **spatial cueing paradigm** to
measure this. A participant fixes their gaze on the centre of a screen. A cue appears, for example an arrow pointing
left or right, and shortly afterwards a target appears on one side, to which the participant responds as quickly as
possible. On most trials the cue is **valid**, pointing to where the target will appear; on some it is **invalid**,
pointing to the other side; on others a **neutral** cue gives no information.

{{diagram cueing | **Figure 2.1** The spatial cueing paradigm. A valid cue speeds the response to a target relative to a neutral cue (a benefit); an invalid cue slows it (a cost). Times are illustrative of the pattern, not values from a particular study.}}

Responses to validly cued targets are faster than after a neutral cue: a **benefit**. Responses to invalidly cued
targets are slower than after a neutral cue: a **cost**. The board's claim, that a wrong cue makes you slower than no
cue at all, is precisely Posner's cost. Attention had been moved to the wrong place and had to be withdrawn and
redirected.

Cues come in two kinds. **Endogenous** cues, such as an arrow or an instruction, are interpreted and followed
voluntarily, and their effect builds over a few hundred milliseconds. **Exogenous** cues, such as a sudden flash at a
location, capture attention automatically and quickly, whether or not they predict anything. This is why a flashing
light is so hard to ignore even when you know it is irrelevant.

## 2.3 Search {#search}

{{board 2F SEARCH}}

In a **visual search** task, a participant looks for a target among distractors, and the researcher varies the number
of items in the display, the **set size**. Anne Treisman and Garry Gelade (1980) found two strikingly different
patterns.

{{diagram search | **Figure 2.2** Feature search and conjunction search. On the left, the target differs from every distractor in a single feature (colour) and pops out. On the right, it differs only in a combination of features, and must be searched for.}}

In **feature search**, the target differs from all distractors in a single feature, such as a red item among green
ones. Search time barely changes as the set size grows: the target seems to leap out of the display, an effect
called **pop-out**. In **conjunction search**, the target is defined by a combination of features, such as a red X
among red Os and green Xs. Now search time increases steadily with set size, as if the items were being checked one
at a time.

Treisman's **feature integration theory** explains the difference. Simple features such as colour and orientation are
registered automatically and in parallel across the whole visual field. Binding features together into a single
object requires attention to be directed to that object's location, one location at a time. The board's final line,
"the odd one out finds you", describes feature search exactly. Later work, notably Jeremy Wolfe's guided search
theory (1994), showed that the two patterns are ends of a continuum: the parallel registration of features is used to
guide attention toward likely candidates, so that most real searches lie between the extremes.

## 2.4 Switching {#switching}

{{board 2F SWITCHING}}

As long ago as 1927, Arthur Jersild found that students working through a list of numbers were slower when they had to
alternate between two operations (adding to one number, subtracting from the next) than when they performed the same
operation throughout. The extra time is the **switch cost**. Rogers and Monsell (1995) showed that it shrinks when
people are given time to prepare for the coming switch, but that it does not disappear: some part of reconfiguring for
a new task cannot be done in advance.

What looks like doing two things at once is usually something else. In the **psychological refractory period**
paradigm, two stimuli arrive in quick succession, each requiring its own response. The closer together they arrive,
the more the second response is delayed. Harold Pashler (1994) argued that this reflects a central **bottleneck**:
the stage at which a response is selected can serve only one task at a time, so the second task waits.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · Talking While Driving]{.bh}
Strayer and Johnston (2001) asked participants to perform a simulated driving task while conversing on a mobile
telephone. During conversation, participants missed more traffic signals and responded more slowly to the signals
they did detect. Hands-free telephones produced the same impairment as hand-held ones. Listening to the radio or to a
book on tape did not. The difficulty was not in the hands but in the conversation, which competed with driving for
the same limited attention.
:::

## 2.5 Missed {#missed}

{{board 2F MISSED}}

**Inattentional blindness** is the failure to notice a fully visible but unexpected object when attention is engaged
elsewhere. Arien Mack and Irvin Rock (1998) studied it systematically with brief displays. The most famous
illustration came a year later.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · Gorillas in Our Midst]{.bh}
Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris (1999) filmed two teams passing basketballs and asked viewers to count the passes
made by the team in white. Midway through the video, a person in a gorilla costume walked into the middle of the
game, faced the camera, thumped its chest and walked off. Roughly half of the viewers did not notice the gorilla at
all. Many who were later shown the video again were astonished, and some insisted that it must have been a different
video.
:::

A related failure, **change blindness**, is the failure to notice a change to a scene, especially when the change
coincides with an interruption. In a field study by Simons and Levin (1998), an experimenter asked a pedestrian for
directions; during the conversation, two people carrying a door passed between them, and the experimenter was
replaced by a different person. About half of the pedestrians did not notice that they were now talking to someone
else.

The board's last line is supported by research too. People are confident that they would notice such events, and
greatly overestimate how often they would; Levin and colleagues (2000) called this misplaced confidence **change
blindness blindness**. The finding connects attention to the last floor of the building: being sure that one saw
everything is not evidence that one did.

### Chapter Summary {#att-summary}

- Attention is limited. Early filter theories gave way to attenuation and capacity theories; on all of them, more
  attention to one thing means less for others (2.1).
- A valid cue speeds responses and an invalid cue slows them relative to no cue; voluntary and automatic cues differ
  in speed and control (2.2).
- A target differing in a single feature pops out regardless of set size; a target defined by a conjunction must be
  searched for (2.3).
- Switching between tasks has a cost that preparation reduces but does not remove; apparent multitasking is usually
  rapid alternation through a central bottleneck (2.4).
- Unexpected objects and changes are frequently missed when attention is engaged, and people underestimate how often
  this happens (2.5).

# Memory {#memory}

{{placard 3F}}

::: objectives
[Learning Objectives]{.bh}

1. Describe memory as encoding, storage and retrieval, and explain the evidence that memories are reconstructed.
2. Describe the shape of the forgetting curve and the evidence that forgetting is never quite complete.
3. Distinguish proactive from retroactive interference and explain the role of similarity.
4. Explain how recall can alter a memory, with evidence from misinformation and reconsolidation research.
5. State the spacing effect and the testing effect, and explain why learners tend to doubt them.
:::

Psychologists divide remembering into three stages: **encoding**, the process by which information is taken in and
transformed into a form that can be kept; **storage**, the keeping of it over time; and **retrieval**, bringing it
back when it is needed. A failure of memory can occur at any of the three. This chapter is concerned mainly with
**long-term memory**, the durable store of facts, events and skills, and its central lesson is the one the placard
announces: memory changes each time it is taken out.

## 3.1 Storing {#storing}

{{board 3F STORING}}

It is tempting to imagine memory as a recording that can be replayed. The evidence points elsewhere. Frederic
Bartlett (1932) asked English participants to read a Native American folk tale, *The War of the Ghosts*, and to
reproduce it from memory, repeatedly, over intervals of minutes to years. Their versions grew shorter, lost details
that seemed strange to them, and were reshaped to fit their own expectations: unfamiliar elements were replaced with
familiar ones, and puzzling events were given explanations the original did not contain. Bartlett concluded that
remembering is **reconstructive**. What is stored is not a copy but an organised outline, which he called a
**schema**, together with a few details, and the memory is rebuilt from them at recall.

The gist survives better than the surface. Jacqueline Sachs (1967) had people listen to passages and then judge
whether a test sentence had appeared in them. After a short delay, people readily noticed a change in meaning but
often failed to notice a change in wording that preserved the meaning. The exact words had been let go; the meaning
had been kept.

What gets kept depends on how it was encoded. Craik and Lockhart's (1972) **levels of processing** framework proposed
that information processed for its meaning (deep processing) is better remembered than information processed for its
appearance or sound (shallow processing). In a classic test, Craik and Tulving (1975) found that words judged for
whether they fitted a sentence were remembered far better than words judged for whether they were printed in capital
letters. Storing, in the board's sense, is building the materials from which a memory can later be built again; the
deeper the building, the better the later reconstruction.

## 3.2 Fading {#fading}

{{board 3F FADING}}

The first rigorous experiments on memory were performed by Hermann Ebbinghaus on himself, and published in 1885. To
avoid the influence of meaning, he memorised lists of **nonsense syllables** such as *DAX* and *BUP*, and measured
forgetting by the **savings** method: how much less time it took to relearn a list to perfection than it had taken to
learn it the first time. Savings could reveal memory even when he could recall nothing.

{{diagram forgetting | **Figure 3.1** Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve: the savings in relearning time after delays from twenty minutes to a month. Most of the loss occurs in the first hours; after that, it slows and levels off.}}

The resulting **forgetting curve** has exactly the shape the board describes. The loss is steepest at first, more than
half of the savings disappearing within a day, and then becomes slower and slower. Murre and Dros (2015) repeated the
experiment more than a century later and obtained a closely similar curve. Mathematically, forgetting is better
described by a power function than by a simple exponential decay (Wixted & Ebbesen, 1991), which captures the
continuing slowdown.

Forgetting also rarely reaches zero. Harry Bahrick (1984) tested people who had studied Spanish at school between one
and fifty years earlier. Their knowledge declined over the first three to six years and then remained remarkably
stable for decades before a late decline, a durable residue he called the **permastore**. Never quite to nothing is a fair summary of the
evidence.

## 3.3 Crowding {#crowding}

{{board 3F CROWDING}}

Is forgetting simply fading with time, or are memories pushed out by other memories? John McGeoch (1932) argued for
the second view: time itself does nothing, any more than time rusts iron; it is what happens during the time that
matters. The best-known support came from Jenkins and Dallenbach (1924), whose participants recalled more after a
period of sleep than after an equal period awake, when new experiences could intervene.

The pushing runs in both directions, as the board says. In **retroactive interference**, new learning impairs memory
for older material: after learning a second list, recall of the first suffers. In **proactive interference**, old
learning impairs memory for newer material. Benton Underwood (1957) showed how powerful proactive interference can be.
Reviewing many studies, he found that the amount a participant forgot of a list over a day depended heavily on how
many lists that participant had learned before it in the laboratory. Much of what had looked like forgetting over
time was interference from earlier learning.

Interference depends on similarity. Material that resembles what was learned before interferes most, because the cues
that should retrieve one item now retrieve its rivals as well. Wickens, Born and Allen (1963) showed the converse in
short-term memory: over a run of brief recall trials with items of one kind, such as consonants, recall fell from
trial to trial; when the items switched to a different kind, such as digits, recall recovered sharply, a **release
from proactive interference**. The most alike are the most easily confused; a change
of kind brings relief.

::: {.box .tryit}
[Try It]{.bh}
Learn the telephone extension of a new colleague, then of another, then of a third. Now recall the first. Next,
learn a fourth number that belongs to something quite different, such as a locker combination, and notice how much
more easily it stays separate. The first set competed; the last did not.
:::

## 3.4 Recall {#recall}

{{board 3F RECALL}}

If memory is reconstructed, every act of recall is an opportunity for it to change. Elizabeth Loftus has shown how
easily this happens.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · Smashed or Hit?]{.bh}
Loftus and Palmer (1974) showed participants films of car accidents and asked how fast the cars were going when they
"hit" each other, or when they "smashed into", "collided with", "bumped" or "contacted" each other. The verb changed
the estimates: "smashed" produced the highest speeds, "contacted" the lowest. A week later, participants who had been
asked the "smashed" question were more likely to report having seen broken glass, although there had been none in the
film. A single word in a question had become part of the memory of the event.
:::

This **misinformation effect** shows that information encountered after an event can be incorporated into the memory
of the event itself. The board describes the mechanism: recall builds a version of the memory, and the version is
saved over the old.

Neuroscience has found a biological counterpart. A memory that has been consolidated, made stable, was long thought to
be fixed. Nader, Schafe and LeDoux (2000) showed that in rats, reactivating a consolidated memory returned it to a
fragile state, in which blocking protein synthesis in the brain could disrupt it. Retrieved memories, they argued,
must be stored again, a process called **reconsolidation**, and during that process they can be modified.

The board's last line is borne out by studies of vivid memories of public events. Talarico and Rubin (2003) asked
students about the September 11 attacks the day after they occurred, and again weeks or months later. The
consistency of their memories declined over time, just as the consistency of their memories for ordinary events did,
but their confidence in the attack memories remained high. With repeated retelling, what people remember is
increasingly their previous rememberings.

::: {.box .caution}
[A Closer Look · How Far Does It Go?]{.bh}
Research on altered and false memories has had real consequences in courtrooms and therapy, and has also been
debated. It is well established that details of real memories can be altered by later information. Whether entire
events can be implanted, and how often, is more contested: studies such as Loftus and Pickrell's (1995) "lost in the
mall" found that a minority of participants came to report partial memories of a suggested childhood event, and later
reviews disagree about how such reports should be counted. The safe conclusion is the board's: recall is a building,
not a reading, and what is built can differ from what was there.
:::

## 3.5 Spacing {#spacing}

{{board 3F SPACING}}

The **spacing effect** is the finding that study distributed over time produces better long-term retention than the
same amount of study massed together. Ebbinghaus noticed it in his own learning, and it has since been confirmed
in hundreds of experiments. A meta-analysis by Cepeda and colleagues (2006) reviewed over three hundred experiments
and found that distributed practice reliably outperformed massed practice, and that the optimal gap between study
sessions grows with the length of time over which the material must be kept.

A second, related finding is the **testing effect**, or retrieval practice effect: practising recall strengthens
memory more than re-reading does. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students study a prose passage and then either
study it again or take a recall test on it. Tested after five minutes, the re-studiers did slightly better. Tested
after a week, the students who had practised recall did substantially better.

The board's last sentence may be its most important. Learners consistently misjudge these effects. Massed study feels
effective because the material is fluent and familiar at the end of the session, and that fluency is mistaken for
learning. Kornell (2009) had students learn vocabulary with flashcards in spaced or massed schedules. Spacing was more
effective for the great majority of participants, yet most of them believed that massing had served them better. Bjork
(1994) called conditions such as spacing **desirable difficulties**: they make learning feel harder while making it
last longer.

::: {.box .tryit}
[Try It]{.bh}
Take two short lists of unfamiliar words. Study the first seven times in one sitting. Study the second once a day
for seven days. A week after the last session, test yourself on both. Most people predict the first list will win.
:::

# Categories {#categories}

{{placard 4F}}

::: objectives
[Learning Objectives]{.bh}

1. Contrast the classical view of categories with prototype and exemplar views.
2. Describe evidence for typicality effects and graded category membership.
3. Explain why the features chosen for sorting determine which things are grouped together.
4. Describe the basic level of categorisation and how expertise shifts it.
5. Explain the role and the warning signs of residual categories.
:::

To **categorise** is to treat different things as equivalent for some purpose: to recognise this animal as a dog,
this object as a chair, this situation as an emergency. Categories let us generalise from what we know about some
members to members we have never met. For two thousand years, the dominant account was the **classical view**,
traceable to Aristotle: a category is defined by a set of **necessary and sufficient conditions**. Every member has
all the defining features, and anything with all of them is a member. The research of the twentieth century showed
that most everyday categories do not work this way.

The first doubt came from philosophy. Ludwig Wittgenstein, in *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), asked what all
games have in common. Some involve winning and losing, some do not; some involve skill, some luck; some are played
alone. He concluded that there is no single shared feature, only "a complicated network of similarities overlapping
and criss-crossing", which he called **family resemblance**.

## 4.1 Middles {#middles}

{{board 4F MIDDLES}}

Eleanor Rosch (1975) asked people to rate how good an example each member of a category was. Agreement was high.
A robin was rated an excellent example of a bird; a chicken less so; a penguin or an ostrich a poor one. These
**typicality** ratings predicted performance in many tasks. People verify that a robin is a bird faster than that a
penguin is a bird (Rips, Shoben & Smith, 1973); asked to list members of a category, they name typical members first
and most often; and children learn typical members of categories earlier.

**Prototype theory** explains these effects by proposing that a category is represented by its **prototype**, a
summary of the category's central tendency, and that an item is categorised by its similarity to the prototype. An
alternative, **exemplar theory** (Medin & Schaffer, 1978), proposes that people store many individual examples and
categorise a new item by its similarity to the stored examples. Both explain the board's observation that a category
has a middle and that everything else is more or less like it. They differ in what the middle is: a summary on one
account, a dense cluster of remembered cases on the other.

## 4.2 Edges {#edges}

{{board 4F EDGES}}

If categories have middles, what happens at their boundaries? William Labov (1973) showed people line drawings of
containers that varied gradually in the ratio of width to depth and asked them to name each one. Narrow containers
were called cups, wide ones bowls, but there was no sharp line between them, and the boundary moved with context.
When participants imagined the container holding coffee, more drawings were called cups; when they imagined it
holding mashed potatoes, more were called bowls.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · Unsure, and Inconsistent]{.bh}
McCloskey and Glucksberg (1978) asked people whether each of a long list of items belonged to categories such as
furniture or fruit. For clear members and clear non-members, people agreed with one another and with themselves. For
borderline items (is a bookend furniture?), they disagreed with one another, and when asked again a month later, many
changed their own answers. The edges of everyday categories are not merely unknown; they are not fixed.
:::

The law has had to draw such lines by decree. In *Nix v. Hedden* (1893), the Supreme Court of the United States had
to decide whether tomatoes were fruits or vegetables for the purposes of a tariff on vegetables. It acknowledged that
botanically a tomato is a fruit, and ruled that in common speech, and therefore for the tariff, it is a vegetable.
A category used for one purpose drew its edge in one place; a category used for another drew it elsewhere.

## 4.3 Features {#features}

{{board 4F FEATURES}}

Sorting depends on which features are considered. A sorter attending to wings will put birds, bats and butterflies
together; a sorter attending to how the young are fed will put bats with mice and whales. Neither is wrong as a
sorting; they serve different purposes. Whales were long classed with fish on the basis of their shape and habitat,
and it was only when naturalists such as Carl Linnaeus attended to other features, warm blood, lungs, and the suckling
of young, that they were moved to the mammals in his *Systema Naturae* of 1758.

Rosch and Mervis (1975) showed that the features of natural categories are not randomly distributed. Typical members
share many features with other members of their category and few with members of contrasting categories; that is
what makes them typical. Features also come in correlated bundles: things with feathers tend to have wings and
beaks, and the mind exploits such correlations.

Similarity alone, however, cannot be the whole story, because almost any two things are similar in some respect.
Murphy and Medin (1985) argued that categories are held together by people's **theories**, their background knowledge
of why features go together. A person who sees someone at a party jump fully clothed into a swimming pool may
categorise them as drunk, not because jumping into pools is a feature of drunkenness but because their theory of
drunkenness explains the act. The features that matter are the ones a theory makes relevant. Pick the wrong ones, as
the board warns, and very different things share a drawer.

## 4.4 Levels {#levels}

{{board 4F LEVELS}}

The same object can be called a kitchen chair, a chair, a piece of furniture or a thing. These names form a
**taxonomy**, a hierarchy in which each category includes those below it. Rosch and her colleagues (1976) found that
one level is psychologically privileged. At the **basic level** (chair, dog, car), categories are most informative for
the least effort: their members share many features with one another and differ clearly from the members of
neighbouring categories. Above them, **superordinate** categories (furniture, animal, vehicle) have members that share
few features; below them, **subordinate** categories (kitchen chair, beagle, saloon car) differ only slightly from
their neighbours.

{{diagram levels | **Figure 4.1** Three levels of one taxonomy. The basic level, in the middle, is the level at which objects are named most readily, learned first by children and recognised fastest.}}

The basic level is the one at which people name objects spontaneously, at which children first learn names, and at
which pictures are identified fastest. Hierarchies also show up in timing. Collins and Quillian (1969) found that
people take longer to verify that a canary is an animal than that a canary is a bird, as if travelling up a hierarchy
of categories one level at a time, although later work showed that typicality and familiarity explain much of that
pattern too.

The basic level is not fixed. Tanaka and Taylor (1991) found that experienced bird-watchers and dog experts
identified pictures in their area of expertise as quickly at the subordinate level (a robin, a beagle) as at the basic
level. Expertise moves the privileged level down the hierarchy.

## 4.5 Other {#other}

{{board 4F OTHER}}

No categorisation scheme fits the world perfectly, and every practical scheme therefore ends with a **residual
category**: *other*, *miscellaneous*, *not elsewhere classified*. In their study of classification systems, Bowker and
Star (1999) examined, among others, the International Classification of Diseases, and showed how residual categories
are both indispensable and revealing. They are where the scheme admits that its categories did not anticipate
everything.

Jorge Luis Borges satirised the arbitrariness of classification in a 1942 essay describing an imaginary Chinese
encyclopedia that divides animals into, among other kinds, those belonging to the Emperor, those drawn with a very
fine camel-hair brush, those that have just broken the flower vase, and *et cetera*. The philosopher Michel Foucault
(1966) said that the passage made him laugh, and then made him think about how every culture's order of things is
only one order among many.

The board's warning is practical. A residual category that grows quickly is information. It signals that the world
contains a kind the scheme was not built to recognise, or that the features chosen for sorting are no longer the
right ones. Classification systems are revised, in libraries, in medicine and in biology, most often when their
*other* drawer has grown too large to ignore.

# Learning {#learning}

{{placard 5F}}

::: objectives
[Learning Objectives]{.bh}

1. Describe the neuron doctrine and the synapse, and explain what is meant by the strength of a synaptic connection.
2. State Hebb's postulate and describe long-term potentiation as evidence for it.
3. Explain the firing threshold, summation, and why cells differ in how readily they fire.
4. Explain why feedback is necessary for skill, with evidence from classic and modern studies.
5. Describe synaptic pruning and sensitive periods in development.
:::

In everyday speech, **learning** means acquiring knowledge or skill. Psychologists define it more broadly, as a
relatively lasting change in behaviour or knowledge that results from experience. The placard adds the question this
chapter answers: if a mind changes itself, what physically changes? The answer, established over the last century and
a half, is that experience alters the connections between the brain's cells, one small join at a time.

## 5.1 Connections {#connections}

{{board 5F CONNECTIONS}}

In the late nineteenth century, it was disputed whether the nervous system was a single continuous web or was made of
separate cells. Using a staining method invented by Camillo Golgi, the Spanish anatomist Santiago Ramón y Cajal showed
that it is made of discrete cells, the **neurons**, which touch without fusing. This is the **neuron doctrine**. Golgi
and Cajal shared the 1906 Nobel Prize for their work on the structure of the nervous system, although Golgi used his
Nobel lecture to reject the doctrine. In 1897 the physiologist Charles
Sherrington gave the point of contact between two neurons its name, the **synapse**.

A typical neuron receives signals through branching **dendrites** and sends its own signal along a single **axon**,
whose terminals form synapses on other cells. The human brain contains roughly eighty-six billion neurons (Azevedo et
al., 2009), and a single neuron may form thousands of synapses. When a neuron fires, its terminals release chemical
messengers, **neurotransmitters**, that alter the electrical state of the receiving cell. At an **excitatory synapse**
the message pushes the receiving cell toward firing; at an **inhibitory synapse** it holds the cell back. In the
brain's outer layer, glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter and GABA the main inhibitory one.

What the board calls the strength of a join, physiologists call **synaptic strength** or synaptic efficacy: how large
a change one firing of the sending cell produces in the receiving cell. Strengths differ from synapse to synapse, and,
crucially, they change.

## 5.2 Practice {#practice}

{{board 5F PRACTICE}}

In *The Organization of Behavior* (1949), the Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb proposed that when one cell repeatedly
takes part in firing another, some growth process or metabolic change takes place in one or both cells, so that the
first becomes more efficient at firing the second. Groups of cells strengthened in this way would come to act
together as **cell assemblies**, the physical basis, Hebb suggested, of an idea. The proposal is known as **Hebb's
postulate**, or Hebbian learning. Its popular summary, "cells that fire together wire together", is usually credited to
the neuroscientist Carla Shatz (1992), who used it to describe how connections are refined in the developing visual
system.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · Long-Term Potentiation]{.bh}
Hebb's postulate waited a quarter of a century for direct physiological evidence. Bliss and Lømo (1973) stimulated a
pathway into the hippocampus of anaesthetised rabbits with brief, high-frequency bursts. Afterwards, single test
pulses along the same pathway produced a larger response in the receiving cells than before, and the increase lasted
for hours. This **long-term potentiation** (LTP), a lasting increase in synaptic strength produced by intense
activity, became the most studied cellular mechanism of learning and memory.
:::

Work on simpler animals showed the same principle at the level of single synapses. Eric Kandel's studies of the sea
slug *Aplysia*, for which he shared the Nobel Prize in 2000, traced simple forms of learning to changes in the
strength of identified synapses, and showed that lasting memories require the synthesis of new proteins and the
growth of new synaptic connections.

Practice changes structure on a larger scale too. Maguire and colleagues (2000) found that licensed London taxi
drivers, who spend years learning the city's streets, had a larger posterior hippocampus than comparison
participants, and the size was related to their years of driving. At the level of behaviour, Fitts and Posner (1967)
described skill acquisition in three stages: a **cognitive stage**, in which the learner thinks through each step; an
**associative stage**, in which errors fall and the steps join together; and an **autonomous stage**, in which the
skill runs with little attention.

## 5.3 Threshold {#threshold}

{{board 5F THRESHOLD}}

A neuron at rest holds an electrical charge across its membrane, its **resting potential**, of about -70 millivolts
(the inside negative relative to the outside). Signals arriving at excitatory synapses push this potential upward;
signals at inhibitory synapses push it down. If the potential at the start of the axon rises past a **threshold**,
typically around -55 millivolts, the cell fires an **action potential**: a brief, self-propagating reversal of charge
that travels down the axon. The action potential is **all-or-none**. A stronger push does not produce a bigger
action potential; it produces one, or several in quicker succession, or none.

{{diagram threshold | **Figure 5.1** Summation and the threshold. Single excitatory pushes (left) raise the membrane potential but fall short. Pushes arriving close together in time add up (right), cross the threshold, and the cell fires.}}

Because a single synapse rarely moves the cell far enough by itself, the cell's decision depends on **summation**.
In **temporal summation**, several signals arriving at one synapse in quick succession add up before the first has
faded. In **spatial summation**, signals arriving at different synapses at the same moment add up. Inhibitory signals
subtract. The cell, in effect, adds its pushes and fires only when the sum crosses the line, exactly as the board
says. The ionic basis of the action potential was worked out by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley (1952) in the giant
axon of the squid, work for which they shared the Nobel Prize in 1963.

Cells differ in how close to the line they sit. A neuron whose resting potential lies nearer its threshold, or whose
threshold is lower, will fire in response to small inputs that leave its neighbours silent. This **excitability** is
not fixed. It is adjusted by **neuromodulators** such as acetylcholine and noradrenaline, which change how readily
whole populations of cells respond, and it can itself change with experience, a process called **intrinsic
plasticity** (Zhang & Linden, 2003). Learning, then, can alter not only how hard one cell pushes another but how
easily each cell is moved at all.

## 5.4 Feedback {#feedback}

{{board 5F FEEDBACK}}

Edward Thorndike (1898) placed hungry cats in **puzzle boxes** from which they could escape, and reach food, only by
operating a latch. Over many trials, the movements that opened the box became more frequent and the useless ones
dropped away. Thorndike summarised the result in the **law of effect**: responses followed by a satisfying outcome
become more likely to recur, and those followed by an unsatisfying one less likely. The outcome is information, and
without it there is nothing to select.

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · Lines Drawn Blindfolded]{.bh}
In a series of experiments reported in *Human Learning* (1931), Thorndike had blindfolded participants draw lines
intended to be a set length, thousands of times over several days, without being told how close they came. Their
accuracy did not improve. Trowbridge and Cason (1932) repeated the task with groups that were told the size of their
error after each attempt, and those groups improved steadily. Repetition alone had produced consistency, a habit, but
not accuracy. Only practice informed by its results produced skill.
:::

Modern research on expertise reaches the same conclusion. Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) found that the best
violinists at a music academy had accumulated far more hours of **deliberate practice** than less accomplished
students: practice designed to improve specific weaknesses, with immediate feedback and opportunities to correct.
Merely playing, however often, did not have the same effect.

::: {.box .caution}
[A Closer Look · Not All Feedback Helps]{.bh}
It does not follow that any feedback improves performance. In a large meta-analysis, Kluger and DeNisi (1996) found
that feedback interventions improved performance on average, but that in over a third of cases they made performance
worse. Feedback directed at the task, and at how to do it better, tended to help; feedback that drew attention to the
person, such as praise or criticism of the learner, often did not. Learning needs to find out how it went, but what
it finds out must be about the work.
:::

## 5.5 Pruning {#pruning}

{{board 5F PRUNING}}

The developing brain overproduces connections and then removes many of them. Peter Huttenlocher (1979) counted
synapses in human frontal cortex at different ages and found that synaptic density rises rapidly after birth, peaks
in early childhood, and then declines through later childhood and adolescence toward adult levels. Huttenlocher and
Dabholkar (1997) found that the timing differs across the brain: synaptic density in the visual cortex peaks within
the first year, while in the prefrontal cortex the decline continues into adolescence. This **synaptic pruning** is
selective. Connections that are used are stabilised; those that are not are eliminated.

Pruning makes development sensitive to experience during particular windows, known as **sensitive periods** (or, when
the window closes sharply, critical periods). David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel showed in the 1960s that if one eye of a
kitten was kept closed during an early period of life, the connections from that eye to the visual cortex lost out
to the connections from the open eye, and the effect persisted; the same closure in an adult cat had little effect.
They shared the Nobel Prize in 1981.

Human perception shows the same pattern. Werker and Tees (1984) found that infants of six to eight months could
distinguish speech sounds used in languages they had never heard, such as contrasts from Hindi and from a language of
the Pacific Northwest, while infants of ten to twelve months raised in English-speaking homes largely could not. The
older infants had not failed to learn; they had specialised in the sounds their own language uses.

The board compresses this into a striking sentence, and it deserves one qualification. Growing up does mean losing
connections on purpose, and in some regions synaptic density falls by something like a third to a half between early
childhood and adulthood. Whether that amounts to "most" depends on the region and the measure. The principle is not in
doubt: a developing brain is shaped as much by what it removes as by what it adds.

# Bias {#bias}

{{placard 6F}}

::: objectives
[Learning Objectives]{.bh}

1. Define a cognitive bias and distinguish it from random error.
2. Explain the framing effect, with medical and public-policy examples.
3. Describe anchoring and explain why it persists when the anchor is known to be arbitrary.
4. Describe confirmation bias and biased assimilation of evidence.
5. Explain hindsight bias, and identify common informal fallacies in argument.
:::

A **cognitive bias** is a systematic departure from the judgment that the evidence warrants. The word that matters is
*systematic*. Random error scatters in every direction and tends to cancel out over many judgments; a bias leans one
way, so that more judgments make it more visible rather than less. Herbert Simon (1955) argued that human reasoning
is **boundedly rational**: it works with limited time, information and attention, and so relies on shortcuts. In a
landmark paper, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman (1974) described several such shortcuts, which they called
**heuristics**, and showed that each produces characteristic biases. This chapter describes five of the best
documented.

## 6.1 Framing {#framing}

{{board 6F FRAMING}}

The board's example comes from medicine. McNeil, Pauker, Sox and Tversky (1982) asked patients, physicians and
students to choose between surgery and radiation therapy for lung cancer. Some were told the outcomes of surgery in
terms of survival (of 100 people having surgery, 90 survive the treatment period); others were told the same
outcomes in terms of mortality (10 die). Surgery was considerably more popular when its outcomes were described as
survival, and the effect was as strong among physicians as among patients. The numbers were identical. The
description chose.

This is the **framing effect**: different but logically equivalent descriptions of the same options lead to different
choices. Tversky and Kahneman (1981) gave the best-known example. Participants imagined that an unusual disease was
expected to kill 600 people and chose between two programmes. When the programmes were described in terms of lives
saved (200 people will be saved for certain, or a one-third chance that all 600 will be saved), most chose the
certain option. When the same programmes were described in terms of deaths (400 people will die for certain, or a
two-thirds chance that all 600 will die), most chose the gamble.

Kahneman and Tversky's **prospect theory** (1979) explains the pattern. People evaluate outcomes as gains or losses
relative to a reference point, and a frame sets that point. They tend to avoid risk when choosing among gains and to
seek risk when choosing among losses, and losses weigh more heavily than equivalent gains, a property known as
**loss aversion**.

## 6.2 Anchors {#anchors}

{{board 6F ANCHORS}}

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · The Wheel of Fortune]{.bh}
Tversky and Kahneman (1974) spun a wheel of fortune, numbered from 0 to 100, in front of participants. In the version
Kahneman (2011) later described, the wheel was rigged to stop at either 10 or 65. Participants were asked whether the percentage of African countries in the United
Nations was higher or lower than the number on the wheel, and then to estimate the actual percentage. Participants who
had seen 10 gave a median estimate of 25 per cent; those who had seen 65 gave a median of 45. A number everyone had
seen chosen by chance had pulled every estimate toward itself.
:::

**Anchoring** is the tendency for an initial value to bias subsequent numerical estimates toward it. Tversky and
Kahneman attributed it to **insufficient adjustment**: people start from the anchor and adjust away from it, but stop
too soon. Later research added a second mechanism, **selective accessibility**: considering whether the answer could be
as high (or as low) as the anchor brings to mind information consistent with that possibility (Strack & Mussweiler,
1997).

Expertise does not protect against anchoring. Northcraft and Neale (1987) found that estate agents' valuations of a
house were pulled toward the listing price they had been given, though most denied that the price had influenced
them. Englich, Mussweiler and Strack (2006) found that experienced judges' sentencing decisions in a hypothetical case
were influenced by a sentencing demand they had determined themselves by throwing dice. The board is right on both
counts: the first number pulls, and it pulls even when everyone knows it was picked at random.

## 6.3 Confirming {#confirming}

{{board 6F CONFIRMING}}

**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to seek, interpret and remember information in ways that support what one
already believes (Nickerson, 1998). Peter Wason studied it with two famous tasks.

::: {.box .tryit}
[Try It · The 2-4-6 Task]{.bh}
The sequence *2, 4, 6* follows a rule. Propose other sequences of three numbers, and for each you will be told
whether it fits the rule. When you are confident, state the rule. (Have a friend play the role of Wason, with the rule
written down in advance: *any three numbers in increasing order*.) In Wason's (1960) study, most participants proposed
sequences such as *8, 10, 12* and *20, 22, 24*, each of which fitted their hypothesis of "numbers increasing by two",
received a yes each time, and announced the wrong rule with confidence. Few tried a sequence that their hypothesis
predicted would fail, such as *1, 2, 3*, which would have revealed the truth.
:::

In Wason's **selection task** (introduced in 1966; Wason, 1968), participants see four cards and must decide which to turn over to test a rule
such as "if a card has a vowel on one side, it has an even number on the other." Most choose the cards that could
confirm the rule and neglect the one that could refute it. Only a small minority choose correctly.

Confirmation bias also shapes how evidence is weighed. Lord, Ross and Lepper (1979) gave supporters and opponents of
capital punishment the same two studies, one apparently supporting and one apparently undermining its deterrent
effect. Each side rated the study that agreed with it as more convincing, found flaws in the other, and ended more
confident in its original view. This **biased assimilation** is the board's second sentence: what disagrees is checked
harder. Ditto and Lopez (1992) found that people required more evidence before accepting an unwelcome conclusion than
a welcome one, a pattern they called **motivated scepticism**.

## 6.4 Hindsight {#hindsight}

{{board 6F HINDSIGHT}}

**Hindsight bias** is the tendency, once an outcome is known, to see it as having been more predictable than it was.
Baruch Fischhoff (1975) gave participants an account of a little-known historical conflict, the war between the
British and the Gurkhas of Nepal in 1814, and told different groups different outcomes. Whichever outcome a group was
told, its members judged that outcome to have been the most likely, and believed they would have predicted it.
Fischhoff called this **creeping determinism**: knowledge of the outcome creeps into the reconstruction of what could
have been foreseen.

Hindsight bias affects memory as well as judgment. Fischhoff and Beyth (1975) asked people to estimate the
probabilities of possible outcomes of President Nixon's visits to China and the Soviet Union before the visits took
place. Afterwards, asked to recall their estimates, they remembered having given higher probabilities to the events
that had occurred.

The bias matters wherever decisions are judged after the fact. A good decision can turn out badly, and a bad one well;
judging decisions by their outcomes, **outcome bias**, punishes the first and rewards the second (Baron & Hershey,
1988). A useful corrective is to **consider the opposite**: before concluding that an outcome was inevitable, list the
ways it could have gone differently (Lord, Lepper & Preston, 1984).

## 6.5 Fallacies {#fallacies}

{{board 6F FALLACIES}}

A **fallacy** is an argument that seems valid or persuasive but is not. Aristotle catalogued fallacies in *On
Sophistical Refutations*, and the subject has been studied ever since (Hamblin, 1970). **Formal fallacies** are errors
in an argument's logical structure. The board lists three **informal fallacies**, which are errors of content or
relevance.

- **Ad hominem** ("to the person"): attacking the person who makes an argument rather than the argument. Whether a
  claim is true does not depend on the character of the person who states it.
- **False dilemma** (or false dichotomy): presenting two options as the only ones when others exist. "Either we ban it
  or we accept chaos."
- **Appeal to popularity** (*argumentum ad populum*, the bandwagon fallacy) and its close relation, the **appeal to
  common practice**: concluding that something is true or acceptable because many people believe or do it.

Recognising fallacies is easier in other people's arguments than in one's own. Pronin, Lin and Ross (2002) found that
people readily rated others as more susceptible to a range of biases than they were themselves, a pattern they
called the **bias blind spot**. Learning the names of biases does not, by itself, remove them.

::: {.box .caution}
[A Closer Look · Are Biases Always Errors?]{.bh}
Some researchers argue that heuristics are often well suited to the environments in which they are used, and that
laboratory tasks can make them look worse than they are (Gigerenzer, 1996). Klayman and Ha (1987) showed that testing
cases one expects to succeed, the strategy that fails in the 2-4-6 task, is often an effective strategy when rules
are narrow. The debate is about how to interpret the biases, not about whether the effects occur. The effects
described in this chapter have been replicated many times.
:::

# Error {#error}

{{placard 7F}}

::: objectives
[Learning Objectives]{.bh}

1. Explain the idea that perception and learning are driven by prediction, and define prediction error.
2. State the principle of error-driven learning and describe blocking as evidence for it.
3. Explain why the size of each correction matters, and describe the consequences of overcorrection.
4. Describe evidence that errors followed by feedback improve learning.
5. Distinguish confidence from accuracy, and describe overconfidence and calibration.
:::

The last floor of the building teaches that being wrong is where the change happens. The idea has a long history and
a precise modern form. The mind continually anticipates what will happen next; when what happens differs from what
was expected, the difference, the **prediction error**, is the signal that drives learning. When nothing unexpected
happens, there is nothing to learn.

## 7.1 Surprise {#surprise}

{{board 7F SURPRISE}}

In the nineteenth century, Hermann von Helmholtz (1867) argued that perception involves **unconscious inference**:
the brain does not simply receive sensations but interprets them, drawing conclusions about their causes from past
experience. Contemporary **predictive processing** accounts develop the idea. On these accounts the brain continually
generates expectations about incoming signals and passes forward chiefly the part of the signal it did not expect,
the error (Clark, 2013).

The brain's response to the unexpected can be measured directly. In the **oddball paradigm**, a listener hears a
regular sequence of identical tones interrupted occasionally by a different one. Even when the listener is reading a
book and ignoring the sounds, the deviant tone evokes a distinctive electrical response, the **mismatch negativity**,
first described by Näätänen, Gaillard and Mäntysalo (1978). The brain registers the violation of its expectation
whether or not it is attending.

The board's definition is exact: surprise is the size of the miss. The bigger the gap between what was expected and
what occurred, the greater the surprise, and, as the next section shows, the greater the learning.

## 7.2 Correction {#correction}

{{board 7F CORRECTION}}

In 1972 Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner proposed a rule for how animals learn to associate a cue with an outcome,
which became one of the most influential ideas in the psychology of learning. The **Rescorla-Wagner rule** states that
the change in the strength of an association on each trial is proportional to the prediction error: the difference
between the outcome that occurred and the outcome that all present cues predicted.

{{diagram prediction | **Figure 7.1** Error-driven learning. On early trials the outcome is unexpected and the error is large, so the association grows quickly. As the outcome becomes predicted, the error shrinks and learning slows.}}

The rule explains why learning curves are steep at first and then level off: early on, the outcome is surprising and
each trial produces a large correction; later, the outcome is expected and each trial changes little. It also
explains a striking phenomenon discovered by Leon Kamin (1969).

::: {.box .spotlight}
[Research Spotlight · Blocking]{.bh}
Kamin first trained rats to expect a mild shock after a noise. He then presented the noise together with a light,
followed by the same shock. Finally he tested the light alone. The rats showed almost no learning about the light,
although it had been paired with the shock many times. The noise already predicted the shock fully, so the shock was
not surprising, and without surprise there was no learning about the new cue. This **blocking** effect is exactly
what an error-driven rule predicts, and what a rule based on mere pairing does not.
:::

Neurophysiology later found a signal with the same properties. Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) reported that
dopamine neurons in the monkey brain fire in response to an unexpected reward. Once a cue reliably predicted the
reward, the neurons fired to the cue and no longer to the reward itself; and when an expected reward failed to
arrive, their activity dipped below its usual level at the moment the reward should have come. The neurons were
signalling not reward but **reward prediction error**.

## 7.3 Steps {#steps}

{{board 7F STEPS}}

The Rescorla-Wagner rule includes a factor that sets how much of each error is corrected on a single trial, which in
animal learning reflects the salience of the cue and the outcome. If that factor is small, learning is slow but
steady. If it is large, each trial produces a large change, and when outcomes vary from trial to trial, large changes
chase each fluctuation, overshooting in one direction and then the other. Correcting by a fraction of each error,
many times, settles closer to the truth than correcting the whole error at once.

The same principle appears wherever people adjust to feedback. In **prism adaptation**, studied by Helmholtz himself,
a person wearing prisms that shift the visual field to one side at first points to the side of targets, then corrects
gradually over many attempts. When the prisms are removed, the person errs in the opposite direction, an
**aftereffect** that shows the correction had been absorbed into the movement itself, and that it, too, must be undone
gradually.

Teaching uses small steps deliberately. In **shaping**, described by B. F. Skinner (1953), a complex behaviour is
built by reinforcing **successive approximations**, each a small step closer to the goal, rather than waiting for the
complete behaviour to appear.

::: {.box .caution}
[A Closer Look · Correcting for Luck]{.bh}
Overcorrection is especially likely when an outcome is partly due to chance. Kahneman (2011) describes flight
instructors who believed that praise for an excellent landing was followed by a worse one, and criticism for a poor
landing by a better one. In fact both followed from **regression to the mean**: an unusually good or bad performance
is usually followed by a more ordinary one, whatever is said in between. A learner who makes a large correction after
every unusually bad result is correcting for luck, and will overshoot.
:::

## 7.4 Mistakes {#mistakes}

{{board 7F MISTAKES}}

It seems natural that making mistakes during learning would be harmful, since the mistake might be remembered instead
of the right answer. A body of research suggests otherwise, provided the mistake is followed by correction. In the
**generation effect**, information that learners produce themselves is remembered better than information they simply
read (Slamecka & Graf, 1978). Kornell, Hays and Bjork (2009) went further: participants who attempted to guess the
answer to a question before being shown it remembered the answer better than those who studied the answer for the
same total time, even when their guesses were wrong. Attempting an answer before studying, **pretesting**, improves
learning of the material that follows (Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009).

The brain registers its own mistakes rapidly. When people make an error in a speeded task, a distinctive electrical
signal, the **error-related negativity**, appears within about a tenth of a second of the incorrect response, often
before the person is aware of having erred (Falkenstein et al., 1991; Gehring et al., 1993).

Noticing is the condition. A mistake that is never noticed, because no feedback arrives, teaches nothing; Chapter 5
showed that practice without knowledge of results produces habit, not skill. A mistake that is noticed carries
exactly the information a given answer does not: where the learner's own expectation was wrong, and by how much
(Metcalfe, 2017).

## 7.5 Certainty {#certainty}

{{board 7F CERTAINTY}}

Confidence is a feeling about a judgment, not a measurement of it. A person is well **calibrated** if their confidence
matches their accuracy: of all the answers they are 70 per cent sure of, about 70 per cent are right. Research on
calibration, reviewed by Lichtenstein, Fischhoff and Phillips (1982), found that people are usually
**overconfident**, especially on difficult questions. In one classic procedure, participants give a range they are
98 per cent sure contains the true value of some quantity; their ranges miss the true value far more often than two
per cent of the time (Alpert & Raiffa, 1982).

Overconfidence is not confined to novices. Philip Tetlock (2005) collected thousands of predictions about political
and economic events from experts over many years. Their accuracy was modest, and the experts who were most certain,
those who explained everything through one big idea, were the least accurate; those who drew on many ideas and
qualified their judgments did better. Kruger and Dunning (1999) reported that people with the least skill in a domain
tended to overestimate their performance most, because the skill needed to perform well is also the skill needed to
recognise good performance; the size and interpretation of that effect are still debated.

The board's final sentence follows from the chapter. If learning is driven by surprise, and surprise is the gap
between expectation and outcome, a person who is certain will seek no test of their belief and will register no
error, and so will not learn. Interestingly, when highly confident people *are* shown to be wrong, the large surprise
makes the correction especially memorable, a **hypercorrection effect** (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001). Certainty does
not prevent learning from a correction; it prevents looking for one.

# Conclusion: What Happens to a Piece of Information {#conclusion .unnumbered}

## The Seven Floors Together {#together}

Each chapter of this book has treated one subject, as each floor of the school does. Read in order, they describe a
single journey.

A piece of information is first **named**. Language gives it a handle, assembled from pieces, set in an order, and
fixed to one meaning by its context (Chapter 1). It is then **noticed**, or not: attention, limited and directed in
advance, selects a small part of what is present and lets the rest pass unseen (Chapter 2). What is noticed may be
**kept**, not as a copy but as materials for rebuilding, which fade, compete with one another, and change with each
recall (Chapter 3). What is kept is **sorted** into categories that have middles but no clean edges, at levels of
generality, by features that some theory made relevant (Chapter 4). Experience **changes** the brain itself, join by
join, as connections used together strengthen and those unused are removed (Chapter 5). Judgment is **bent** by the
way options are framed, by anchors, by the wish to be right, and by knowledge of how things turned out (Chapter 6).
And finally the whole system is **corrected**, because the gap between what was expected and what occurred is the
signal that drives learning (Chapter 7).

The chapters also speak to one another. Names shape what is remembered (1.1, 3.1). Attention determines what can be
learned (2.5, 7.4). Recall rebuilds memory, and confidence in a rebuilt memory can outrun its accuracy (3.4, 7.5).
Categories decide which features are noticed (4.3, 2.3). Feedback is the condition of skill (5.4) for the same reason
that prediction error is the engine of learning (7.2). And biases persist partly because they rarely produce the
noticed error that would correct them (6.5, 7.5).

## Studying for the Examination {#studying}

The school publishes its examination's terms in advance. Each floor's syllabus states that the examination covers the
board and that the pass mark is 80 out of 100. The research in this book suggests how to prepare.

1. **Space your study** (3.5). Visit each chapter's boards on several days rather than all at once.
2. **Practise retrieval** (3.5). Close the book and recall each board in your own words before checking it.
3. **Attempt before you look** (7.4). Answer each chapter's review questions before rereading the chapter.
4. **Mind the alike** (3.3). Topics that resemble one another, such as proactive and retroactive interference, are
   the most easily confused; study them side by side and name the difference.
5. **Check your calibration** (7.5). For each answer you give in practice, note how sure you are, and see whether your
   confidence matches your accuracy.
6. **Welcome the wrong answers** (7.2, 7.4). A noticed mistake shows you exactly where your expectation was wrong.
   That is the most useful thing a practice test can give you.

## A Last Word {#last-word}

The board on the top floor ends with the warning that nothing surprises the surest, so they learn the least. It is a
fitting end to an introduction. Every finding in this book was once a surprise to someone who expected otherwise and
took the trouble to check. The habit of checking, of treating one's own certainty as a hypothesis, is the most
durable thing a course on the mind can teach.


# Glossary {#glossary .unnumbered}

::: glossary
**action potential** The brief, all-or-none electrical signal a neuron sends along its axon when its membrane
potential crosses threshold. (5.3)

**ad hominem** A fallacy that attacks the person making an argument instead of the argument. (6.5)

**agent** The thematic role of the one who performs an action. (1.3)

**anchoring** The pull of an initial value on subsequent numerical estimates. (6.2)

**arbitrariness** The absence of any natural resemblance between a word's form and its meaning. (1.1)

**attention** The processes by which some information is selected for further processing at the expense of other
information. (Ch. 2)

**attenuation** Treisman's proposal that unattended input is weakened rather than blocked. (2.1)

**basic level** The level of a taxonomy (e.g., chair) at which categories are most informative for the least effort.
(4.4)

**bias blind spot** The tendency to see biases in others more readily than in oneself. (6.5)

**biased assimilation** Evaluating evidence as stronger when it supports one's prior view. (6.3)

**blocking** The failure to learn about a new cue when the outcome is already predicted by another cue. (7.2)

**bottleneck** A processing stage that can serve only one task at a time. (2.4)

**calibration** The match between a person's confidence and their accuracy. (7.5)

**change blindness** The failure to notice a change to a scene, especially across an interruption. (2.5)

**cognitive bias** A systematic departure from the judgment that the evidence warrants. (Ch. 6)

**confirmation bias** The tendency to seek, interpret and remember information in ways that support existing beliefs.
(6.3)

**conjunction search** Search for a target defined by a combination of features; time grows with set size. (2.3)

**cross-modal priming** A method in which a spoken word's effect on recognition of a visually presented word is
measured. (1.4)

**deliberate practice** Practice designed to improve specific weaknesses, with immediate feedback. (5.4)

**desirable difficulties** Conditions, such as spacing, that make learning feel harder but make it last longer. (3.5)

**encoding** Taking information in and transforming it into a form that can be stored. (Ch. 3)

**endogenous cue** A cue that directs attention voluntarily, such as an arrow. (2.2)

**error-related negativity** An electrical brain signal that follows an erroneous response within about 100
milliseconds. (7.4)

**exemplar theory** The view that a category is represented by stored individual examples. (4.1)

**exogenous cue** A cue that captures attention automatically, such as a sudden flash. (2.2)

**fallacy** An argument that seems valid or persuasive but is not. (6.5)

**false dilemma** A fallacy that presents two options as the only ones when others exist. (6.5)

**family resemblance** A pattern of overlapping similarities among category members, with no feature shared by all.
(Ch. 4)

**feature search** Search for a target differing from distractors in a single feature; it pops out. (2.3)

**forgetting curve** The relation between time since learning and amount retained: steep at first, then levelling.
(3.2)

**framing effect** Different choices produced by logically equivalent descriptions of the same options. (6.1)

**generation effect** Better memory for information produced by the learner than for information read. (7.4)

**Hebb's postulate** The proposal that a connection strengthens when one cell repeatedly takes part in firing another.
(5.2)

**heuristic** A mental shortcut that usually works but produces characteristic biases. (Ch. 6)

**hindsight bias** Seeing an outcome, once known, as more predictable than it was. (6.4)

**hypercorrection effect** The finding that high-confidence errors, once corrected, are especially well corrected.
(7.5)

**inattentional blindness** Failure to notice a visible but unexpected object while attending elsewhere. (2.5)

**interference** Impairment of memory by other learning; proactive (old on new) or retroactive (new on old). (3.3)

**law of effect** Thorndike's principle that responses followed by satisfying outcomes become more likely. (5.4)

**levels of processing** The proposal that deeper, meaning-based processing produces better memory. (3.1)

**long-term potentiation** A lasting increase in synaptic strength following intense activity. (5.2)

**loss aversion** The tendency for losses to weigh more heavily than equivalent gains. (6.1)

**misinformation effect** The incorporation of information encountered after an event into memory of the event. (3.4)

**mismatch negativity** The brain's electrical response to a deviant sound in a regular sequence. (7.1)

**morpheme** The smallest unit of language that carries meaning. (1.2)

**neuron** A nerve cell; the discrete unit of the nervous system. (5.1)

**orthographic depth** The consistency with which a writing system maps sounds to letters. (1.5)

**overconfidence** Confidence that exceeds accuracy. (7.5)

**permastore** Bahrick's term for knowledge that survives for decades after initial decline. (3.2)

**polysemy** Related senses carried by a single word. (1.4)

**pop-out** The immediate detection of a target that differs in a single feature. (2.3)

**prediction error** The difference between what was expected and what occurred. (Ch. 7)

**productivity** The capacity of language to produce and understand new expressions from known parts. (1.2)

**prospect theory** Kahneman and Tversky's account of choice in terms of gains and losses from a reference point.
(6.1)

**prototype** A summary representation of a category's central tendency. (4.1)

**reconsolidation** The restabilising of a memory after retrieval, during which it can be modified. (3.4)

**reconstructive memory** The view that remembering rebuilds an event from stored materials. (3.1)

**regression to the mean** The tendency of an extreme outcome to be followed by a less extreme one. (7.3)

**Rescorla-Wagner rule** The principle that associative learning on each trial is proportional to prediction error.
(7.2)

**residual category** A category for what fits no other, such as *other* or *miscellaneous*. (4.5)

**retrieval** Bringing stored information back into use. (Ch. 3)

**schema** An organised body of knowledge used to interpret and reconstruct experience. (3.1)

**sensitive period** A period in development during which experience has an especially strong effect. (5.5)

**shaping** Building a behaviour by reinforcing successive approximations to it. (7.3)

**spacing effect** Better long-term retention from study distributed over time than from massed study. (3.5)

**summation** The adding up of inputs to a neuron, across time (temporal) or across synapses (spatial). (5.3)

**switch cost** The extra time required when changing from one task to another. (2.4)

**synapse** The junction at which one neuron passes a signal to another. (5.1)

**synaptic pruning** The developmental elimination of unused synapses. (5.5)

**synaptic strength** How large a change one firing of a sending cell produces in a receiving cell. (5.1)

**syntax** The rules by which words combine into phrases and sentences. (1.3)

**testing effect** Better retention from practising recall than from re-studying. (3.5)

**threshold** The membrane potential at which a neuron fires an action potential. (5.3)

**typicality** How good an example of its category an item is judged to be. (4.1)

**unconscious inference** Helmholtz's term for the interpretation of sensations by drawing on past experience. (7.1)
