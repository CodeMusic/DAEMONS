Take a moment before reading this article to look around, and think about everything you see. Have you every wondered if what you see if the same as what someone else sees? How about something like a hue: is the red I see the same red you see? All these questions can be easily answered, but not until we appreciate the differences between sensation, and perception. With an understanding of these two terms the answer not only becomes simple, but their relationship will reveal something incredible. This revelation will not only help explain why people behave as they do, but also improve your ability to communicate in ways which produce drastically more effective results.

 Before discussing sensation and perception lets first talk about information, and a simplified example of recognizing something, maybe your family pet. The first part of this process is when the light (information) comes into your eye, focuses on your retina, and travels into your brain. The second part of this process is when that information is analyzed, resulting is recognition (of say, your dog spot). In this part of the process all your memories and experiences come into play. These memories begin to describe the raw information that your eyes received. Since all of this happens within a fraction of a second, we don’t even know it is happening! To us, we look and we see; however, there is much more happening ‘underneath the hood’. The two terms used to describe each part of this are: ‘bottom-up’, and ‘top-down’ information/processing. The basic information your eyes (or any other sense organ) gathers is known as the bottom-up information, and all your memories which come into play during the analysis is called top-down information. Eventually those two types of information interact and the result is recognition.

 Now, lets return to the more familiar terms of sensation and perception in order to put these pieces together. Sensation is the initial experience resulting from processing the ‘bottom-up information’ of what your eyes (or other sense organs) experience. Sensation is like a very basic description which acts to prime the what comes next. After your sense organs deliver the sensation deeper into the brain those patterns trigger thoughts, memories, and emotions all which progressively describe and result into our perception. For the next part of the discussion lets consider how various beliefs could influence perception.

 Sensation and perceptions are so entwined that we cannot clearly experience a sensation without it being subject to some form of perception. I should also mention that this process is much more complicated than I described, though to assist with learning we give labels to what is happening at different steps along the way. It would be more accurate to say information flows from sensation to perception, however, by clearly defining these two stages of the process we can address the more philosophical questions, such as the ones I posed earlier. The important part to understand is the magnitude in which perception can be different from its initial sensation. A very crude analogy for this would be like looking at the world from a distance and describing it as a circle, while it is true it is not the entire picture. You have to experience the world before gaining a perception of it, and further more if you were to compare multiple perceptions of the world you learn that everyone describes it differently.

 Now that we understand the basics of sensation and perception something powerful has undoubtedly been revealed. We all experience, and interact through our perception of the world, and since perception is born from our memories and emotions it is unique to us. Now lets address the philosophical question regarding the experience of hue. For this lets disregard certain genetic abnormalities of the eye, such as color blindness, and consider a normally functioning adult human eye.

 When sensation and perception enter the scene all of these can easily be answered! Lets take a momentary break from vision and answer the age old riddle: ‘If a tree falls in the woods and no one is around to hear it, does it make a sound?’. Well, since sound is a perception and perception requires an observer then the answer is no. An unobserved falling tree does, however, produce vibrations of energy which are capable of vibrating an observers ear drum, but technically speaking, no observer, no sound. Without an observer sound cannot exist, only fluctuations in air pressure.

 To address the second question posed, ‘could my red be your blue but we just use the same word?’ we will briefly touch on the components of light colour, and how this relates to those who are colour blind. Within you retina you have two type of photoreceptors, rods and cones; these different receptors line the inside of your eye (retina). Light focused on a specific area of your retina will activate a specific cell. When an image is focused on the retina a certain amount of processing actually happens right within your eye! Though for this articles simplicity lets just think of your eyes ability of detecting merely detecting the presence of light pixel. Rods are more sensitive to light, but they do not detect colour. Also, rods are most concentrated in your peripheral vision (the outer areas of the retina); this is why when it is dark it is easier to recognize things when you do not look directly at them. Cones on the other hand are more concentrated in the foci (centre) of your eye. For the cone, presence of light does not merely activate the receptors. A cone contain three primary pigments, and respond to colour. Cones absorbs the light and responds to the specific wavelengths of red, green, and blue (note although I am using the colour’s name here, they receptors are mechanically activated by the electromagnetic vibrations which resonate to the specific pigment within the cone). People who are colour blind (various type of colour blindness) have an error within their DNA causing a cone photoreceptor with an incorrect pigment. Although this article is not intended to be about biology it is still important to recognize that the bottom-up information relating to a normal cone within the eye will respond the same the same way to any frequency of light . So, a cone (colour sensitive photoreceptor) will respond the same way to red light for everyone; just as a cone will respond the same way to blue light for everyone. Just as the receptors response to blue will be different than its response to red. To summarize, in a normally working human eye the sensation of red is the same for everyone. In fact the sensation of every specific hue or colour is the same for everyone. 

 In the beginning as asked a question, ‘is the red I see the same as the red you see?’, and just like with the classic tree falling riddle the keyword is a perception word (in this case ‘see’, in the other ‘sound’). Although the initial information received will produce very similar sensations for everyone, that is not what we experience. So, do you experience the same red that I experience? No. The red I experience is influenced by every other red I have experienced, all of my thoughts about it, my experiences, beliefs, and knowledge all influence how the red appears to me. Some people associate love towards red, some anger, and although this varies greatly from person to person it varies even more between cultures. Although I have used an example of a hue this applies to every recognizable form, from simple (shapes, hues, smells, tastes, sounds, etc) to the very complex (people, accents, nature, paintings, music, etc), as well as every combinations of these. To put it simply your experience is unique to you! When you notice the beauty of colours and elegant movements of a trees leaves, don’t take that for granted. While nature provided you with the bottom-up information, that incredible experience was produced specifically for you, and it incorporates billions of bits of information not only from your past, your current state of mind, but also from your hopes and dreams of the future. The present moment is a precious symphony perfectly conducted for one specific person, you!

 To recap the highlights that we have discussed: Two people looking at the same thing may receive the same information through their eyes, but their experience of that information will be vastly different. This not only applies to visions, but to all of our sense organs. When we start discussing how this affects the perception of language, due to its imperfect nature things vary even more. Phrases will often have a different understanding within different people. More so, even the words we use will associate to different memories and emotions to another person. All in all, if we speak from within our comfort zone there is a great chance what you say will be differently understood in another.

 I love this quotation by Alan Greenspan, and feel it captures this part of the article. Eventually when get to know someone well it becomes easier to communicate with them, and you can have a greater confidence that they understand you. Regarding people you do not know very well, how can we better connect to people with them?, or how can you better prepare the big presentation you have approaching? Knowing a bit about you audience is a huge factor because your goal is to find out what you have in common in order to draw from those resources. Discussions are easier because we have the advantage of feedback, but even without verbal feedback do not under estimate the power of subtle feedback. While someone may not tell you to stop using unfamiliar terms with their words, their eye gestures and expressions will share powerful insight to whether or not you are building rapport. Another powerful tool is listening, not just in general but also to the common words that they use. Words that we may know may, however, be unfamiliar to others. This source of confusion can be minimized by using phrases and words which they tend to make use of. That being said, you still want to present your unique self; however, by finding a common ground you can drastically accelerate the amount of rapport built. Remember, people like to feel understood, and the more you can sincerely relate to someone the more effective and easier communication will become. Lastly, I want to mention a persons favourite word, especially to someone you have just met; that word is their name. Remembering a persons name can be challenging; however, taking that extra effort not only makes a person feel more comfortable, but you will often find that they become more open during a discussion. By understanding the degree of influence that perception uniquely creates within everyone, will not only help you better understand your daily experience, but it will also open the door to clearer expression, communication, and the reception of your ideas.
